You are working on the mechanical CAD and 3D-printing portion of a small drone project. Focus ONLY on CAD, mechanical design, printability, PETG, assembly, dimensions, tolerances, and physical testing. Do not work on software, electronics, flight-control code, computer vision, radio control, simulation, or networking.

## Hardware available for printing

We have:

- Monoprice Voxel 3D printer
- Build volume: approximately 150 × 150 × 150 mm
- 0.4 mm nozzle
- 1.75 mm PETG filament
- Heated bed
- PETG is the intended material for essentially all prototype structural parts

Because the printer is limited to a 150 mm build area, anything significantly larger must be split into printable modules.

## What we are building

The drone will use approximately 2-inch propellers.

The planned protective cage is approximately:

- 245–250 mm overall diameter/width
- Significantly larger than the Voxel's print bed
- Therefore it CANNOT be one print

The immediate priority is a FIXED protective cage.

A two-ring movable/gimbal-style cage may be developed later, but do not allow that design to compromise or delay the fixed cage.

The fixed cage must remain a usable standalone configuration even if a later gimbal system is developed.

## Primary mechanical goal

Design a lightweight, modular PETG cage that:

1. Protects the propellers during low-energy contact with walls, objects, or targets.
2. Prevents the propellers from being the first point of contact.
3. Can survive repeated light impacts.
4. Is light enough for a small 2-inch drone.
5. Can be printed entirely on the Monoprice Voxel.
6. Can be repaired by replacing individual printed pieces instead of reprinting the entire cage.
7. Can be assembled/disassembled repeatedly.
8. Keeps expensive drone hardware protected.
9. Uses simple hardware and printed components wherever practical.
10. Is easy to modify as real-world measurements become available.

## Required modular architecture

Do NOT create a monolithic 245–250 mm cage.

Design it as separate components.

A reasonable starting architecture is:

Fixed Cage Assembly
- Front-left cage section
- Front-right cage section
- Rear-left cage section
- Rear-right cage section
- Replaceable cage-to-cage connectors
- Drone-to-cage mounting brackets
- Replaceable impact/contact pieces
- Fastener retainers or clips where useful

You may change the exact number of cage sections if another segmentation produces a stronger, lighter, or easier-to-print system.

Every individual printed component must fit inside the Voxel's 150 × 150 × 150 mm build volume with reasonable margin.

Do not design parts that technically fit only by using the absolute diagonal of the build volume unless necessary. Prefer easy, reliable print orientations.

## Modularity

Treat the following as separate CAD components whenever practical:

### 1. Main cage sections
These form the protective perimeter.

Requirements:
- individually printable
- replaceable
- lightweight
- stiff enough to maintain prop clearance
- rounded/smooth external contact geometry
- avoid sharp impact points

### 2. Cage connectors
Use separate connectors between cage sections.

These should:
- be cheap and fast to print
- be easy to replace
- maintain alignment
- resist twisting
- preferably fail before expensive drone components are damaged in a severe impact

Consider:
- tongue-and-slot joints
- keyed joints
- dovetail-like joints where appropriate
- bolted overlap joints
- pinned joints
- printed sleeves
- combinations of mechanical alignment plus screws

Do not rely purely on a small amount of PETG snap-fit flex unless testing demonstrates it is reliable.

### 3. Drone-to-cage mounts
Do not permanently integrate the drone mounting geometry into every cage section.

Use separate mounting brackets/adapters.

Goals:
- allow mounting geometry to change without redesigning the complete cage
- allow broken brackets to be replaced
- reduce force transferred directly into the airframe
- provide some sacrificial mechanical behavior

### 4. Contact/bump pieces
If there are obvious regions likely to contact walls or targets, consider small replaceable bump/contact components.

These can be optimized specifically for:
- impact
- abrasion
- quick replacement
- low print time

### 5. Camera/protective accessory mounts
Any small protective mounts should remain independent from the primary structural cage where possible.

Do not make the whole cage dependent on the dimensions of one accessory.

## PETG-specific design requirements

Design specifically for FDM PETG, not injection molding.

Assume an initial printing profile around:

- 0.20 mm layer height
- 0.4 mm nozzle
- 3–4 walls/perimeters for structural pieces
- approximately 20–35% infill as a starting range
- PETG nozzle temperature roughly 230–240°C as an initial range
- bed roughly 55–70°C depending on filament/printer tuning

These are starting points, not immutable values.

Favor walls/perimeters and good geometry over extremely high infill.

Do NOT make structural parts solid unless there is a clear reason.

## Layer orientation

Layer orientation is extremely important.

For every structural part, explicitly determine the intended print orientation.

Avoid arrangements where an impact loads the weakest inter-layer direction directly in tension.

For long cage members:
- prefer printing them lying down where practical
- try to run extrusion paths along the length of the structural member
- avoid making layer adhesion the primary thing resisting bending or impact

For connectors:
- consider load paths from multiple directions
- orient the part so screw loads and impact loads do not simply split the layers

Include recommended print orientation for every part.

## Supports

Minimize supports.

Prefer:
- chamfers instead of unsupported horizontal overhangs
- 45-degree transitions
- teardrop-shaped horizontal holes where useful
- self-supporting geometry
- splitting complicated components instead of filling them with supports

Parts should ideally be printable repeatedly without difficult support removal.

## Tolerances

The Monoprice Voxel is an FDM printer, so do not CAD mating PETG components with zero clearance.

Make fit dimensions parametric.

Start with reasonable prototype clearances, for example:

- approximately 0.2–0.3 mm per side for relatively close fits
- approximately 0.3–0.5 mm per side for easier sliding/removable fits

These should be adjustable parameters rather than hard-coded assumptions.

Create tolerance test pieces before committing to all final joints if appropriate.

## Fasteners

Prefer commonly available small hardware.

Use captured nuts, heat-set inserts, through-bolts, or self-tapping screws only where appropriate.

For early prototypes, simple nuts and bolts may be preferable to heat-set inserts because they are easy to change.

Avoid designs requiring obscure hardware.

Where possible:
- make screw sizes parametric
- provide nut traps
- provide tool access
- prevent hardware from contacting propellers

## Weight

Weight matters significantly because this is going onto a small 2-inch drone.

Do not overbuild the cage.

Every structural element should have a reason to exist.

Use:
- ribs
- curved sections
- hollow/sectioned beams
- thin walls with reinforcement features
- local strengthening only where needed

instead of simply making everything thick.

Provide estimated printed volume/mass where possible.

Assume PETG density around 1.25–1.30 g/cm³ for rough CAD estimates if exact filament density is unavailable.

## Geometry constraints

The cage should be around 245–250 mm overall.

Treat that as the current design target, not an exact immutable dimension.

The cage must maintain safe clearance around:
- propellers
- motors
- airframe
- wires
- antennas
- any mounting hardware

Do not assume exact airframe hole spacing unless real measurements are available.

Use parameters/placeholders for dimensions that still need physical measurement.

Clearly label dimensions that must be measured once hardware is in hand.

## Actual-part measurements

All planned project hardware has already been ordered.

Therefore:

DO NOT redesign purchased hardware.

Design printed components around the physical hardware.

As parts become available, critical interfaces should be measured with calipers.

Dimensions that should eventually come from real measurements include:
- frame dimensions
- mounting hole locations
- motor locations
- maximum propeller envelope
- board/component clearance
- battery clearance
- camera dimensions
- antenna clearance
- screw diameters
- standoff locations

Make the CAD parametric enough that these measurements can be changed without reconstructing the entire cage.

## Fixed cage comes first

The fixed cage is the core mechanical design.

Do not immediately optimize for a gimbal.

The fixed cage should first prove that it can handle:

- at least 10 consecutive low-energy contacts without cage damage causing failure
- no prop strikes during those contacts
- at least 3 minutes of usable flight with the cage attached
- repeatable manual recovery following contact/disturbance

The fixed cage should remain functional and available even after later designs are created.

## Future two-ring gimbal

Only after the fixed cage is mechanically successful should a two-ring/gimbal system become a major CAD task.

If preparing for it now, only make interfaces modular enough that a gimbal can be attached later.

Do not unnecessarily add gimbal complexity to the fixed cage.

A future gimbal system should itself be modular:

Two-Ring Gimbal Assembly
- cage adapter
- outer ring segments
- inner ring segments
- pivot components
- bushings/bearing interfaces
- retainers/stops
- replaceable connectors

Because a ~245–250 mm ring cannot fit on the Voxel, both future rings must also be segmented.

Do not design the rings as impossible single-piece prints.

## Sacrificial design

Where reasonable, intentionally make cheap printed pieces easier to replace than expensive components.

A hard hit should ideally damage:
1. a small replaceable PETG connector or bumper

before damaging:
2. motors
3. frame
4. camera
5. flight electronics

However, sacrificial components should not be so weak that normal low-energy contacts cause constant failures.

## Development sequence

Use this mechanical development order:

1. Establish all major CAD parameters.
2. Create simple tolerance/joint test coupons.
3. Test PETG mating clearances.
4. Design one cage section.
5. Design one connector.
6. Print and mechanically test that section/connector.
7. Adjust thickness, clearance, and joint geometry.
8. Build the full fixed-cage CAD assembly.
9. Print and assemble the fixed cage.
10. Perform fit/clearance checks without propellers.
11. Verify propeller envelope and hardware clearance.
12. Perform low-energy mechanical/contact tests.
13. Iterate weak areas only.
14. Preserve the successful fixed-cage version.
15. Only then begin detailed two-ring gimbal development.
